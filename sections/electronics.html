Section 05 · The Electrons

Electronics & Wiring

Pinouts, channel assignments, power budgeting, and the conventions that will keep your wiring clean once the PCB is soldered.

The Pin Map

Every servo lives on a digital pin. Every pin has a job. Memorise the channel map below; you will be referring to it through every subsequent section of this manual.

Servo Channel Assignment

The eight servos are addressed by leg and joint. The mnemonic is {FL|FR|BL|BR}-{H|K}: front/back, left/right, hip or knee. The PCB headers J2 through J9 follow the order below, left to right when looking at the assembled PCB from above.

J2FL-HFront-LeftHipD20

Note · Why these pins

The Servo library on the ATmega328P uses Timer1 internally and supports up to twelve software-PWM channels on any digital pin. We chose D2–D9 because they're contiguous (easy to remember), avoid the hardware UART pins on D0/D1 (used by USB serial), and leave the PWM-capable pins D10/D11 free for future expansion.

Tip · The "reserved" pins are a hint

If you find yourself wondering why we left A4/A5 (I²C) and several extra digital pins broken out to headers, that's part of what gets revealed in Cebu. Resist the urge to populate them with anything yet.

Power Architecture

This is the most failure-prone area of the entire build. Read the section twice.

Why two rails?

An SG90 idle draws ~10 mA. An SG90 stalled draws ~700 mA. Eight servos stalling at once (say, on a hard landing where every leg tries to brace) can pull just under 4 A in a transient. The Arduino Nano's onboard 5 V regulator can supply at most ~800 mA continuously and will fold under sudden loads.

If you tie the Nano's 5 V pin directly to the servos, you will see the classic failure: the moment servos start moving, the Nano's voltage sags, the ATmega328P browns out, and the firmware locks up. The robot then continues to hold the last commanded servo positions until you cycle power. It's a frustrating bug to diagnose.

The buck converter solves this by stepping 7.4 V down to 5 V at up to 3 A. But that's not actually the fix. The fix is keeping the servo current loop physically separate from the Nano's 5 V pin. The buck output feeds both (the Nano takes its 5 V from one branch, and the servo headers take 5 V from the other), but the servo current never travels along the Nano's traces.

Warning · Do not power servos from the Nano's 5V pin

This is the single most common failure mode in beginner servo projects. The Nano's 5 V pin is connected to its onboard regulator, which is rated for hundreds of milliamps, not amps. Brown-out, lockup, eventual destruction of the Nano are the predictable results.

Decoupling

The two 470 µF electrolytics (C1, C2) sit on the servo rail right at the input to the eight servo headers. They absorb the inrush current when a servo suddenly starts moving and prevent the rail voltage from dropping. The three 100 nF ceramics (C3, C4, C5) handle high-frequency noise: one across the buck output, one across the Nano's 5 V/GND, one across the servo rail. Don't skip any of them.

Servo Cable Conventions

Every SG90 ships with the same three-wire cable. The colours are standardised but read them yourself to confirm:

WireFunctionPCB Header Pin (looking at PCB top, J2–J9)
Brown (or black)GroundPin 1, closest to the PCB edge
Red+5 V (servo rail)Pin 2, middle
Orange (or yellow / white)PWM signalPin 3, closest to the Nano
Warning · Connector polarity

It is mechanically possible to install an SG90 connector on the header backwards. Doing so reverses the polarity into the servo, which kills the SG90 within seconds and may damage the Nano via the signal pin. Confirm the brown wire faces the PCB edge before powering on, every time, especially after re-wiring.

Buck Converter Setup

The MP1584 / LM2596 modules ship configured for whatever the last person left them at: typically 5 V, but not always. You must set them before installing the module on the PCB.

  1. Connect the module to a 9 V battery or bench supply

    Apply 7–9 V to the input terminals. Do not load the output yet; you're configuring it open-circuit, which is fine for setting voltage.

  2. Measure the output voltage

    Probe the OUT+ and OUT– pads with a multimeter. Note the reading.

  3. Adjust the trimpot until OUT = 5.00 V ± 0.05 V

    The blue trimpot rotates many turns. Turn slowly, watching the meter. Lock in 5.00 V; never above 5.20 V (some SG90 batches don't tolerate it well).

  4. Disconnect, mark, install

    Once set, mark the trimpot with a dot of nail polish or paint so you know it's been configured. Then proceed to PCB soldering.

Power Budget Estimate

  • Idle Current~80 mA
  • Walking Avg~600 mA
  • Peak Transient~4 A
  • Pack 1500 mAh~2 hr walk
  • Pack 2200 mAh~3 hr walk

A 1500 mAh 2S pack is the practical minimum. A 2200 mAh pack is what we recommend; the extra capacity costs little and the marginal weight is well within the servo torque budget.

Ready for PCB?

You should now understand: which pin drives which servo, why the servo rail and Nano rail are separated, how to set the buck converter, and what the connector polarity is. If any of that is fuzzy, re-read this page. The next section assumes all of it.
