Every servo is assigned to a digital pin, and every pin has a defined function. Commit the channel map below to memory; it is referenced throughout every subsequent section of this manual.

Buck Converter Setup

The XL4015E1 modules ship configured to whatever setting was last used during manufacturing or testing, typically 5 V, though not always. The output voltage must be set before installing the module on the PCB. This build uses two of them, one for the servo rail, one for the Raspberry Pi rail; repeat the procedure below for each module, independently, rather than assuming the second matches because the first was set correctly.

Setting the Output Voltage

  1. Connect the module to a 9 V battery or bench supply

    Apply 7–9 V to the input terminals. Do not load the output yet; the module is configured open-circuit, which is appropriate for setting voltage.

  2. Measure the output voltage

    Probe the OUT+ and OUT– pads with a multimeter. Note the reading.

  3. Adjust the trimpot until OUT = 5.00 V ± 0.05 V

    The blue trimpot rotates through many turns. Turn slowly while watching the meter. Set precisely to 5.00 V; never exceed 5.20 V, as some SG90 batches do not tolerate higher voltages well.

  4. Disconnect, mark, install

    Once set, mark the trimpot with a dot of nail polish or paint to indicate it has been configured. Then proceed to PCB soldering.

Note · The trimpot takes patience

This is a multi-turn trimpot, not a single-sweep dial. It can take on the order of a hundred turns in one direction before the meter reading moves at all. Do not mistake this for a broken module and stop early; keep turning slowly, in the same direction, and watch the meter rather than the trimpot.

Tip · Substituting a different buck converter

The XL4015E1 is the module specified for this build, but any buck converter may be substituted, provided it outputs a regulated 5 V and is rated for at least 5 A. Confirm both figures before installing a substitute; a module rated below 5 A will brown out under servo load even if it measures correctly on the bench with no load attached.

Note · Why these pins

The Servo library on the ATmega328P uses Timer1 internally and supports up to twelve software-PWM channels on any digital pin. Twelve servos require the use of all of them: D2–D13, contiguous and avoiding the hardware UART pins on D0/D1 (used by USB serial). This leaves no digital pins free for a status indicator, so the status LED is wired directly to the Nano's 3V3 pin instead, simply lighting whenever the Nano is powered rather than reflecting any digital pin state; the analog pins, A0 through A7, carry no function in this design and stay unpopulated.

Power Architecture

Section 01 introduced the shape of this at a glance (Figure 02): the battery feeds two buck converters, one regulated 5 V rail for the servos and PCB, a separate one for the Raspberry Pi, which in turn powers the Nano, camera, and proximity sensor. This section makes that distribution explicit, down to the rail, the capacitor, and the failure mode each one exists to prevent; it is the most failure-prone area of the entire build, so read it carefully.

Warning · No inline power switch

This design has no switch between the battery and either buck converter. The system powers on the instant the battery is connected, both rails together. Make every other connection first, servos seated, Nano socketed, Raspberry Pi ready, before wiring in the battery, and disconnect the battery itself to power everything down.

Power-On Sequence

SW1 sits downstream of Buck #1's regulator, not upstream of it: connecting the battery brings both buck converters up immediately, Pi rail included, whether or not SW1's jumper wires are joined. That gap, servo rail regulated but not yet delivered, is deliberate, and the order below depends on it. Power on in this order, every time:

  1. Connect the battery, leave SW1 open

    With the SW1 jumper wires separated, wire in the battery. Both buck converters come up together: Buck #1's regulated servo rail sits ready at SW1's open contact, unable to reach the servo headers yet, and Buck #2 begins powering the Raspberry Pi.

  2. Wait for the Pi to boot, then check D1

    Give the Raspberry Pi time to finish booting; it powers the Nano over USB once it is up. D1, the status LED, lights the instant the Nano has power, regardless of what firmware it is running. Treat an unlit D1 as the signal to stop: something upstream, the Pi, its USB output, or the Nano's socket, is not powered yet.

  3. Close SW1 only once D1 is lit

    Join the SW1 jumper wires to bring the servo rail online. By this point the Nano is already running and driving a defined PWM signal on every servo header's signal pin.

Warning · Closing SW1 before D1 lights makes the servos squiggle

Closing SW1 while the Nano has no power leaves the servo rail energised with every signal pin floating: nothing is driving them. An SG90 in that state does not sit still, it squiggles, twitching its horn erratically as it reacts to noise on an undriven signal line rather than holding a commanded position. This is not a fault in the servo; it is a symptom of powering the servo rail before the signal source. The fix is the sequence above, not a different servo.

Why two rails?

Section 01 already named the rule: servos must never draw from the Nano's onboard regulator. Here is why that rule is absolute, in numbers. An idle SG90 draws approximately 10 mA; a stalled SG90 draws approximately 700 mA. Twelve servos stalling simultaneously, for instance during a hard landing where every leg attempts to brace, can draw up to roughly 8 A in a transient (12 × 700 mA). The Arduino Nano's onboard 5 V regulator can supply at most ~800 mA continuously and will fail under sudden loads of this magnitude.

Connecting the Nano's 5 V pin directly to the servos produces a characteristic failure: the moment the servos begin moving, the Nano's voltage sags, the ATmega328P browns out, and the firmware locks up. The robot then holds its last commanded servo positions until power is cycled. This failure mode can be difficult to diagnose without prior knowledge of its cause.

Buck #1 steps 7.4 V down to 5 V at up to 5 A continuous, with bulk capacitors on the servo rail absorbing transients above that. This alone, however, is not the complete solution. The essential design principle is keeping the servo current loop physically separate from the Nano's 5 V pin, and this build takes that further than splitting one regulator into two branches: the Nano's own 5 V now comes from the Raspberry Pi, over a dedicated wire, off Rail 1 entirely. Servo current never has a path onto the Nano's supply at all.

Warning · Do not power servos from the Nano's 5V pin

This is the single most common failure mode in beginner servo projects. The Nano's 5 V pin is connected to its onboard regulator, which is rated for hundreds of milliamps, not amps. Brown-out, lockup, and eventual damage to the Nano are the predictable results.

Connecting Buck #2 to the Raspberry Pi

Buck #2's regulated 5 V never touches a header or a screw terminal: it reaches the Pi over a USB-C cable with one end stripped to bare conductors. Cut away whatever was originally on that end, strip back the outer jacket to expose the VBUS (usually red) and GND (usually black) conductors, and solder those two to OUT+ and OUT– on Buck #2. Leave the USB-C plug on the other end intact and plug it into the Raspberry Pi's own USB-C power port, the same port a wall charger would normally use.

Warning · Power the Pi through its USB-C port, never its GPIO pins

Do not wire Buck #2's output to the Pi's 5V/GND GPIO pins instead, even though it looks like a shorter path. The USB-C port carries the Pi's onboard power-path circuitry, under-voltage lockout and reverse-current protection among it, that the GPIO rail bypasses entirely. Injecting power through GPIO removes that protection along with the connector's strain relief, and a wiring mistake there reaches the Pi's silicon directly.

Decoupling

Two 470 µF electrolytics (C1, C2), paralleled with each other, sit on the servo rail at the input to the twelve servo headers. Together they absorb the inrush current when a servo begins moving and prevent the rail voltage from dropping. An earlier board revision also carried a 100 µF electrolytic (C3) at the +5V input from J1, filtering a branch that fed the Nano directly; both that branch and C3 are gone now, since the Nano is powered over USB from the Raspberry Pi instead.

Grounding

Every ground on the PCB, the Nano's GND pin, each servo header's pin 1, the legs of C1 and C2, and the terminal block, is tied to one common net, referenced at buck converter #1. Treat it as a single plane; there is no separate "digital ground" and "servo ground" to keep apart here. The PCB itself now carries just the one regulated rail, for the servos; the Nano's 5 V arrives separately, by wire, from the Raspberry Pi, but the Nano's ground pin stays on this same PCB plane.

The battery's negative lead is the one exception, on both rails. Each buck converter's return runs on its own dedicated wire straight to the battery, rather than joining a common plane at some arbitrary point along the way. This keeps the return path for the battery's raw current short and singular, the same reasoning that keeps servo current off the Nano's traces, applied to the return side of the circuit instead of the supply side.

Warning · Connector polarity

It is mechanically possible to install an SG90 connector on the header backwards. Doing so reverses the polarity into the servo, which destroys the SG90 within seconds and may damage the Nano via the signal pin.
