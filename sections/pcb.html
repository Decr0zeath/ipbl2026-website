Read the Schematic First

A blank PCB has been shipped to each delegate as part of the kit. The board layout is provided; the schematic that produced it is published below. Every component on the bare board should be traceable to a symbol on the schematic before the soldering iron is used.

Note · Two paths through this section

Some delegates may wish to redesign the PCB from the schematic; this is encouraged as a portfolio exercise, but a self-fabricated board should not be brought to Cebu. All teams will work from the standardised PCB during the programme. Soldering practice takes place on the supplied board.

The Schematic

The schematic is divided into four functional sections. Read each one and confirm you can locate its components on the bare PCB. The schematic PDF is in the project repository: quadruped-schematic-v1.pdf.

Section A · Power input and switch

Battery enters the board at J1, a 2-pin screw terminal (5 mm pitch). Positive feeds through SW1, the master power switch, and then into the buck converter's V_IN pin. The negative is tied to the board's common ground plane. SW1 is not a manufactured switch part: it is a break in the trace bridged by two single-pin headers, closed by joining a male-to-male and a male-to-female jumper wire, and opened by separating them.

Warning · No reverse-polarity protection

This board revision has no protection diode on the battery input. Connecting BT1/BT2 backwards at J1 will feed reverse voltage straight into the buck converter with nothing to block it. Confirm polarity with a meter before every connection, and wire the screw terminal the same way every time.

Section B · Buck converter and rails

The buck converter module is not a single chip on the PCB but a daughter-board (XL4015E1 module) that you solder in by its four header pins. A 100 µF capacitor (C1) sits at its input for bulk filtering on the battery side. Its output net is labelled +5V and feeds two physical rails:

  • A low-current +5V_LOGIC branch with a ferrite bead (FB1) in series, feeding the Nano's 5V pin
  • A high-current +5V_SERVO branch going to the twelve servo headers, decoupled by C2 and C3 (470 µF each)

Both branches share ground but the ferrite bead keeps servo switching noise off the Nano's supply.

Section C · Microcontroller

The Arduino Nano sits in a socketed arrangement of two 15-pin female headers, one per row, so the module is not directly soldered, preserving the option to replace it. Its pin mapping is exactly as documented in Section 05. All twelve digital pins D2–D13 go to servo headers, so the status LED (D1) is instead wired straight to the Nano's 3V3 pin via R1 (330 Ω): it lights whenever the board has power and is not firmware-controlled.

Section D · Servo headers

Twelve identical 3-pin 0.1" headers in a row. Pin 1 of each connects to GND; pin 2 to +5V_SERVO; pin 3 to the corresponding Nano digital pin (D2 through D13). The signal traces run with a ground return alongside them on the inner layer where possible, though this is a 2-layer board, so the routing is opportunistic.

Pre-Solder Checks

Before any solder touches the board, complete the following four checks:

  1. Inspect the bare board for defects

    Hold the board up to a light. Look for hairline scratches across traces, missing solder mask, mis-printed silkscreen, or oxidation on the pads. The boards were inspected before shipping but transit damage can happen. Photograph any defect and email the host team before you proceed.

  2. Continuity-test the power rails

    With a multimeter in continuity mode, probe between the battery + terminal and the buck input pad. You should hear a buzz. Now probe between the battery + terminal and the battery – terminal. You should hear nothing. If you hear a buzz across the battery terminals on a bare board, the board is faulty; do not proceed.

  3. Sort components into a tray

    Resistors with resistors, capacitors with capacitors, headers with headers. Read the resistor's colour code or use the meter to confirm it is the 330 Ω before soldering.

  4. Bend leads and pre-form parts

    For the axial through-hole resistor (R1), bend the leads at 90° to match the footprint spacing. This step requires some care but avoids difficulty with springy leads under the iron.

Soldering Order

Solder shortest-profile components first, tallest last. This keeps the board flat when you flip it to solder, with the components held against the table by gravity. The exact order:

Tip · Solder technique on this board

Heat the pad and the lead together for one second. Touch solder to the joint, not the iron. It should flow in, form a small concave fillet, and set within another second. A complete joint should take roughly three seconds end to end. If it is taking longer, the iron is too cold or the flux is exhausted; clean the tip and re-tin.

Polarity-Sensitive Components

The following components will be damaged, or will damage adjacent components, if installed backwards. Verify each one with a meter before applying power.

  1. D1 (LED). Long lead = anode (+). Flat side on plastic body = cathode (–).
  2. C1, C2, C3 (electrolytic capacitors). Long lead = +. White stripe on the can = – side.
  3. The Nano itself. The USB port faces the edge of the PCB labelled "USB"; do not insert it rotated 180°.

Post-Solder Inspection

Before any power is applied to a freshly assembled board, do this:

  1. Visual inspection under good light

    Every joint should look like a shiny, concave fillet, not a dull grey blob. Dull joints are usually "cold"; reflow them. Blob joints often hide a short to a neighbouring pad; flux and re-touch.

  2. Continuity check: battery + to battery –

    With the SW1 jumper wires disconnected, probe across the battery terminals. There must be no continuity. If there is, you have a short somewhere on the input side, most often a solder bridge near J1.

  3. Continuity check: +5V_LOGIC to GND

    Probe the Nano's 5 V pin (in its socket) against GND. There should be no continuity. If there is, you have a short on the 5 V rail; check around the Nano socket pins and C1.

  4. Continuity check: +5V_SERVO to GND

    Probe pin 2 of any servo header against pin 1 of the same header. There should be no continuity. If there is (most commonly because an electrolytic was installed backwards and shorted), disconnect everything and find it before applying power.

  5. Smoke test: bench supply at 7.4 V, current limit to 200 mA

    If you have access to a current-limited bench supply, this is the moment to use it. Set 7.4 V, 200 mA limit, and connect the SW1 jumper wires with no Nano installed and no servos connected. The supply should show roughly 30–50 mA of current draw (the buck converter idle). The buck converter output should read 5.00 V at its output pads. If current goes to limit, disconnect the SW1 wires immediately and find the short.

  6. Install the Nano, repeat smoke test

    Power down. Insert the Nano into its socket with the USB port facing the labelled edge. Power up again; current should rise to about 80 mA (Nano idle plus buck idle). The Nano's onboard power LED should illuminate.

Mounting the PCB to the Chassis

Once smoke-tested and verified, the PCB drops into the chassis on the four standoffs you installed in Section 04. Secure with four M2 × 4 mm screws from above. Route the twelve servo cables into the chassis from the corner cutouts and onto their labelled headers (J2 through J13). The battery exits the chassis through the rear cutout to its external pack.

Warning · Do not power on with servos connected yet

Confirm the firmware is loaded and the servos are centred (in software) before connecting the servo cables to the PCB. Uncentred firmware can swing servos to mechanical extremes the instant power is applied, placing bending forces on the legs that they were not designed to withstand. This is covered in Section 07.
