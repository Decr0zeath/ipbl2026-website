Section 04 · The Build

Mechanical Assembly

Eight servos, four legs, two chassis plates. With patience and a screwdriver this takes three or four hours. With impatience, it takes considerably longer.

Before You Begin

Every leg you build is going to be identical. Build the first one carefully and slowly. Build the next three by repetition. By the fourth leg you'll be quicker than this guide can keep up with — that's the goal.

Warning · Centre your servos first

Before installing any servo into a printed part, you must move it to its mechanical centre (90°). Otherwise you'll mount the horn at an arbitrary angle and discover, three hours later, that one of your legs only travels from 30° to 130° instead of 0° to 180°. Read the "Pre-centring" step below before touching any screws.

Pre-Centring Every Servo

This is a one-time step. It takes about ten minutes for all eight servos and saves several hours of grief later.

  1. Wire one servo to an Arduino at a time

    Use any Arduino, any breadboard, any USB cable. The servo's three wires connect: orange → pin 9, red → 5 V, brown → GND. Power the Arduino from USB; the SG90 will pull just under 200 mA on idle which the USB rail can handle for short bursts.

  2. Upload the centring sketch

    Flash the small sketch servo_center.ino from the project repository. It does exactly one thing: writes 90° to pin 9 and stays there. The servo will swing to its mechanical centre and hold.

  3. Mark the centred shaft

    While the servo is powered and holding at 90°, install one of the supplied servo horns onto the shaft in the orientation you want — typically perpendicular to the body. Mark the shaft and horn with a permanent marker so you can re-align if the horn ever comes off. Do not screw the horn down yet — you'll do that during leg assembly.

  4. Power down, unplug, set aside

    The servo is now mechanically centred. Move on to the next one. Repeat for all eight.

Assembly Sequence

The mechanical build proceeds in this order:

  • A. Build one leg (hip-mount + hip servo + femur + knee servo + tibia)
  • B. Repeat three more times to produce four identical legs
  • C. Mount the lower chassis plate
  • D. Attach all four legs to the chassis
  • E. Mount the upper chassis plate and close up

The PCB and battery are installed later, in §05, after wiring.

A · Building One Leg

  1. Seat the hip servo in the hip mount

    Take one pre-centred SG90 and slide it into the rectangular pocket in the hip mount. The output shaft should face upward when the L-bend points outward. Press until the servo flange sits flush against the print. If it doesn't seat easily, do not force it — file the pocket walls until it does.

  2. Secure the hip servo with two M2 × 8 mm screws

    The screws thread through the servo's mounting tabs and into the printed walls of the hip mount. Tighten until snug — not gorilla-tight. Two screws (one front, one back) is sufficient; the other two holes can be filled later if you have OCD about symmetry.

  3. Install the femur onto the hip-servo output shaft

    The femur has a circular pocket sized for the included single-arm or cross-arm servo horn. Press the horn into the femur pocket and check that it sits flat. Then place the femur (with horn) onto the hip servo's output shaft. With the servo still at 90° (which you set during pre-centring), the femur should point outward, parallel to the ground, perpendicular to the hip-mount L-bend.

  4. Secure the femur to the horn with the supplied M2 × 4 mm

    Use the small black screw that came with the SG90 — not a generic M2. The SG90's included screw is a slightly different thread pitch designed to bite into the horn without splitting it. Drive it down the centre of the horn and into the servo's output shaft. Snug, not crushed.

  5. Seat the knee servo in the femur

    The far end of the femur has a second servo pocket. Slide a second pre-centred SG90 into it with the output shaft facing down and slightly forward (you'll see why in the next step). Secure with two M2 × 8 mm screws.

  6. Attach the tibia to the knee servo

    Insert a servo horn into the tibia's pocket, then place the tibia onto the knee-servo output shaft. With the knee servo still at 90°, the tibia should hang straight down from the femur. Secure with one M2 × 4 mm to the servo shaft.

  7. Manually verify range of motion

    With the servos unpowered, gently swing the leg through its expected motion: hip rotates the femur up and forward; knee rotates the tibia up and back. You're checking that nothing binds, no print rubs against itself, and there's no interference at extremes. If something binds, fix it now — once a servo is powered, it can't tell the difference between binding and a hard mechanical stop, and it will burn out trying.

Note · Cable management at this stage

Each leg now has two servo cables — three wires each, six wires total. Don't tie them down yet. You'll route them up through the hip mount and into the chassis during electronics assembly. Just let them hang for now.

B · Repeat for Three More Legs

Build legs two, three, and four following exactly the same steps. After all four are built, line them up on your bench in a row. They should be visually indistinguishable. If one looks noticeably different — femur angle, tibia hang — go back and find out why. Doing this comparison now is much easier than chasing an asymmetric gait later.

Tip · Label the cables now

While each leg is still in isolation, label its two servo cables with masking tape: FL-H / FL-K, FR-H / FR-K, BL-H / BL-K, BR-H / BR-K (front-left hip, front-left knee, and so on). This label saves you in §05 and again in §07.

C · Lower Chassis Plate

  1. Place the lower chassis plate flat, holes facing up

    Identify the four corner cutouts where the hip mounts attach. Confirm orientation: the rectangular cut-out at one end is for battery access — that's the "rear" of the robot.

  2. Install the four PCB standoffs

    Drive four M2 × 4 mm screws up through the underside of the plate into the M2 brass standoffs above it. These are where the PCB will sit. Finger-tight is fine for now; you'll re-snug after the PCB is installed.

D · Attaching Legs to Chassis

  1. Position the first leg at the front-left corner

    Orient the hip mount so its L-bend points outward (away from the chassis centre). The hip-servo output shaft, with femur, should now be at the corner. Cable bundle routes up into the chassis through the corner cutout.

  2. Secure the hip mount to the lower plate with three M2 × 6 mm screws

    The hip mount sits on top of the lower chassis plate. Three screws form a triangle that resists twisting under servo load. Drive from above, threading into the printed walls of the hip mount below.

  3. Repeat for the remaining three corners

    Front-right, back-left, back-right. After all four legs are attached, the assembly should be able to stand on its own — though the legs will be at whatever angle the unpowered servos happen to settle at. They'll look correct only after the firmware drives them.

E · Closing Up

Do not install the upper chassis plate yet. You need to wire and test the PCB first — see §05 and §06. The upper plate goes on at the very end, in §08.

For now, leave the robot with its lower chassis open. Set it carefully on the bench, legs splayed out at whatever angles the servos chose. Eight servo cables should be poking up out of the chassis cavity.

Mechanical Sign-Off

Before continuing to electronics, confirm all of the following:

  1. All four legs are visually identical, leg length matches within 1–2 mm
  2. No mechanical binding when each joint is moved by hand through its full range
  3. All sixteen leg screws are seated firmly (none stripped, none cross-threaded)
  4. All eight servo cables are labelled with their position
  5. The chassis sits flat on a flat surface (no rocking)

If yes to all five, continue. If no — fix it now. The next sections assume mechanical completion.
