The Machine in One Page

Twelve servos. Four legs. One Arduino Nano. A custom PCB to organise the wiring, a battery to sustain motion, and sustained effort to bring the system to a working state.

The platform is a small quadruped, occasionally referred to as a "spider robot" for its splayed posture, with three degrees of freedom per leg. One servo swings the leg at the coxa (side to side); one lifts it at the hip; one swings it forward and back at the knee. With careful timing across the twelve joints, the robot can produce a basic crawl, trot, and turn-in-place. The robot does not respond to anything: the main sketch running on the Nano steps through a fixed set of states, and the gait plays out on its own once power is applied. This is the full extent of this manual's aim: a robot that stands and walks forward reliably. More advanced behaviour is reserved for Day 01 in Cebu.

System Block Diagram

Before any soldering begins, hold the entire system in mind. There are three subsystems, and you will work within each of them in turn.

Subsystem 01 · Power

A 2S Li-Ion pack (nominally 7.4 V) supplies all power in the system, but the Nano and the servos both accept 5 V maximum, so a step-down stage sits between the battery and every load. This build uses an XL4015E1 buck converter to derive a clean 5 V rail for the Nano and, critically, a separate, higher-current 5 V rail for the servos; any converter that regulates down to 5 V at up to 5 A of output current is an acceptable substitute. Twelve servos drawing current simultaneously represent a non-trivial load; the servos must never be powered from the Nano's onboard regulator, as doing so reliably produces a brown-out.

Subsystem 02 · Control

An Arduino Nano (ATmega328P) sits at the centre of the PCB. It drives the twelve servos via the Servo library, begins the gait automatically a few seconds after boot, and accepts individual servo commands over serial for calibration. The Nano provides ample headroom for a twelve-channel software-PWM task; computational capacity is not a constraint in this design.

Subsystem 03 · Actuation

Twelve SG90 servos, three per leg. The coxa servo (closest to the body) controls sideways swing; the hip servo controls lift; the knee servo controls forward swing. This is a deliberately simple three-degree-of-freedom leg design.

What "Done" Looks Like

By the end of this manual you should be able to demonstrate, on a bench, the following three behaviours:

  1. Power on, stand up. The robot lifts itself into a neutral standing pose within two seconds of receiving power.
  2. Walk forward. A simple four-step gait moves the robot forward by approximately one body length per cycle, in a straight line, on a flat surface.
  3. Individual servo addressability. Through the serial monitor, any of the twelve servos can be commanded to any valid angle.

These three behaviours are the goal to reach before arrival day. Reaching them means you carry a solid grasp of the system into the event, ready for what comes next.

Prerequisites

This manual assumes nothing beyond general working knowledge; specifically, you should already possess, or be prepared to acquire quickly:

  • Comfort with a soldering iron and through-hole soldering
  • Familiarity with the Arduino IDE, having flashed at least one prior "blink" sketch
  • Reading a basic schematic (resistor, capacitor, regulator, pin headers)
  • Basic command of multimeter use: continuity, voltage, resistance
  • Patience for mechanical assembly with small screws and limited tools

If any of these are unfamiliar, particularly soldering, address that gap first. Learning to solder for the first time directly on the PCB makes for a considerably more difficult build.

Safety, in Brief

  1. Solder fumes. Work in a ventilated space. Fume extractors are ideal; an open window is the minimum.
  2. Lithium batteries. Do not short, puncture, or charge above their rated voltage. Use a balance charger.
  3. Servos under load. Twelve servos can produce enough combined torque to bend a leg or pinch a finger. Power off before adjusting any mechanical components.
  4. Soldering irons. Tin the tip, return the iron to its stand, and never test its temperature with your hand.
