The Pin Map

Every servo is assigned to a digital pin, and every pin has a defined function. Commit the channel map below to memory; it is referenced throughout every subsequent section of this manual.

Buck Converter Setup

The XL4015E1 modules ship configured to whatever setting was last used during manufacturing or testing, typically 5 V, though not always. The output voltage must be set before installing the module on the PCB.

  1. Connect the module to a 9 V battery or bench supply

    Apply 7–9 V to the input terminals. Do not load the output yet; the module is configured open-circuit, which is appropriate for setting voltage.

  2. Measure the output voltage

    Probe the OUT+ and OUT– pads with a multimeter. Note the reading.

  3. Adjust the trimpot until OUT = 5.00 V ± 0.05 V

    The blue trimpot rotates through many turns. Turn slowly while watching the meter. Set precisely to 5.00 V; never exceed 5.20 V, as some SG90 batches do not tolerate higher voltages well.

  4. Disconnect, mark, install

    Once set, mark the trimpot with a dot of nail polish or paint to indicate it has been configured. Then proceed to PCB soldering.

Note · The trimpot takes patience

This is a multi-turn trimpot, not a single-sweep dial. It can take on the order of a hundred turns in one direction before the meter reading moves at all. Do not mistake this for a broken module and stop early; keep turning slowly, in the same direction, and watch the meter rather than the trimpot.

Tip · Substituting a different buck converter

The XL4015E1 is the module specified for this build, but any buck converter may be substituted, provided it outputs a regulated 5 V and is rated for at least 5 A. Confirm both figures before installing a substitute; a module rated below 5 A will brown out under servo load even if it measures correctly on the bench with no load attached.

Connect each servo to its header by Nano pin: D2 to M1, D3 to M2, D4 to M3, and so on through D13 to M12. On every header, the orange wire, the signal wire on the servo's female connector, must line up with the header's printed label; wherever that label sits on the board, that is where the orange wire goes.

Warning · M12 sits out of line

M12 is not placed in sequence with the rest of the row; see Figure 10. Locate it by its printed label before connecting, rather than assuming it follows M11 in physical order.

Note · Why these pins

The Servo library on the ATmega328P uses Timer1 internally and supports up to twelve software-PWM channels on any digital pin. Twelve servos require the use of all of them: D2–D13, contiguous and avoiding the hardware UART pins on D0/D1 (used by USB serial). This leaves no digital pins free for a status indicator, so the status LED is wired directly to the Nano's 3V3 pin instead, simply lighting whenever the Nano is powered rather than reflecting any digital pin state; the analog pins, A0 through A7, carry no function in this design and stay unpopulated.

Power Architecture

Section 01 introduced the shape of this at a glance (Figure 02): the battery feeds the buck converter, and the buck converter's regulated 5 V feeds the PCB, which in turn distributes it onward. This section makes that distribution explicit, down to the rail, the capacitor, and the failure mode each one exists to prevent; it is the most failure-prone area of the entire build, so read it carefully.

Why two rails?

Section 01 already named the rule: servos must never draw from the Nano's onboard regulator. Here is why that rule is absolute, in numbers. An idle SG90 draws approximately 10 mA; a stalled SG90 draws approximately 700 mA. Twelve servos stalling simultaneously, for instance during a hard landing where every leg attempts to brace, can draw up to roughly 8 A in a transient (12 × 700 mA). The Arduino Nano's onboard 5 V regulator can supply at most ~800 mA continuously and will fail under sudden loads of this magnitude.

Connecting the Nano's 5 V pin directly to the servos produces a characteristic failure: the moment the servos begin moving, the Nano's voltage sags, the ATmega328P browns out, and the firmware locks up. The robot then holds its last commanded servo positions until power is cycled. This failure mode can be difficult to diagnose without prior knowledge of its cause.

The XL4015E1 buck converter steps 7.4 V down to 5 V at up to 5 A continuous, with bulk capacitors on the servo rail absorbing transients above that. This alone, however, is not the complete solution. The essential design principle is keeping the servo current loop physically separate from the Nano's 5 V pin. The buck output feeds both branches (the Nano draws its 5 V from one branch, the servo headers from the other), but servo current never travels along the Nano's traces.

Warning · Do not power servos from the Nano's 5V pin

This is the single most common failure mode in beginner servo projects. The Nano's 5 V pin is connected to its onboard regulator, which is rated for hundreds of milliamps, not amps. Brown-out, lockup, and eventual damage to the Nano are the predictable results.

Decoupling

The two 470 µF electrolytics (C2, C3) sit on the servo rail at the input to the twelve servo headers. They absorb the inrush current when a servo begins moving and prevent the rail voltage from dropping. A single 100 µF electrolytic (C1) sits at the buck converter's input, on the battery side, providing bulk filtering ahead of the regulator.

Servo Cable Conventions

Every SG90 ships with the same three-wire cable. The colours are standardised, but should be confirmed by inspection:

Warning · Connector polarity

It is mechanically possible to install an SG90 connector on the header backwards. Doing so reverses the polarity into the servo, which destroys the SG90 within seconds and may damage the Nano via the signal pin. Confirm the brown wire faces the PCB edge before every power-on, particularly after re-wiring.
