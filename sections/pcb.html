Read the Schematic First

A blank PCB has been shipped to each delegate in the kit. The board itself is laid out for you; the schematic that drove it is published below. You should be able to trace every component on the bare board to a symbol on the schematic before you reach for the soldering iron.

Note · Two paths through this section

Some of you will want to redesign the PCB yourselves from the schematic; that's encouraged for your own portfolio, but do not bring a self-fabricated board to Cebu. We will be working from the standardised PCB across all teams. Soldering practice happens on the supplied board.

The Schematic

The schematic is divided into four functional sections. Read each one and confirm you can locate its components on the bare PCB. The schematic PDF is in the project repository: quadruped-schematic-v1.pdf.

Section A · Power input and switch

Battery enters the board at J1 (JST-XH or XT30, depending on your pack). Positive feeds through SW1 (the master power switch) and then into the buck converter's V_IN pin. The negative is tied to the board's common ground plane. Note the reverse-polarity protection: a Schottky diode (D2) sits between J1 and SW1; it will pass forward current at the cost of ~0.3 V but block damage if the battery is plugged in backwards.

Section B · Buck converter and rails

The buck converter module is not a single chip on the PCB but a daughter-board (MP1584 or LM2596 module) that you solder in by its four header pins. Its output net is labelled +5V and feeds two physical rails:

  • A low-current +5V_LOGIC branch with a ferrite bead (FB1) in series, feeding the Nano's 5V pin
  • A high-current +5V_SERVO branch going to the eight servo headers, decoupled by C1 and C2 (470 µF each)

Both branches share ground but the ferrite bead keeps servo switching noise off the Nano's supply.

Section C · Microcontroller

The Arduino Nano sits in a socketed female-header arrangement (no direct-soldered Nano; you want the option to swap it out). Its pin mapping is exactly as documented in §05. The reset pin is broken out to a small tact button (RST) for convenience. The status LED (D1) sits on D10 via R1 (330 Ω). The user push-button (SW2) is on D11 with R2 (10 kΩ) as a pull-up to 5 V; pressing the button pulls the pin to ground.

Section D · Servo headers

Eight identical 3-pin 0.1" headers in a row. Pin 1 of each connects to GND; pin 2 to +5V_SERVO; pin 3 to the corresponding Nano digital pin (D2 through D9). The signal traces run with a ground return alongside them on the inner layer where possible, though this is a 2-layer board, so the routing is opportunistic.

Pre-Solder Checks

Before any solder touches the board, do these four things:

  1. Inspect the bare board for defects

    Hold the board up to a light. Look for hairline scratches across traces, missing solder mask, mis-printed silkscreen, or oxidation on the pads. The boards were inspected before shipping but transit damage can happen. Photograph any defect and email the host team before you proceed.

  2. Continuity-test the power rails

    With a multimeter in continuity mode, probe between the battery + terminal and the buck input pad. You should hear a buzz. Now probe between the battery + terminal and the battery – terminal. You should hear nothing. If you hear a buzz across the battery terminals on a bare board, the board is faulty; do not proceed.

  3. Sort components into a tray

    Resistors with resistors, capacitors with capacitors, headers with headers. Read every resistor's colour code or use the meter; the 330 Ω and the 10 kΩ are easy to confuse at a glance.

  4. Bend leads and pre-form parts

    For axial through-hole parts (resistors, the Schottky diode), bend the leads at 90° to match the footprint spacing. This is fussy but saves you fighting with springy leads under the iron.

Soldering Order

Solder shortest-profile components first, tallest last. This keeps the board flat when you flip it to solder, with the components held against the table by gravity. The exact order:

Tip · Solder technique on this board

Heat the pad and the lead together for one second. Touch solder to the joint (not the iron). It should flow in, form a small concave fillet, and reflow within another second. A whole joint should take three seconds end to end. If it's taking longer, your iron is too cold or your flux is exhausted; clean the tip and re-tin.

Polarity-Sensitive Components

Four components on this board will be damaged or will damage other things if installed backwards. Verify each one with a meter before applying power.

  1. D2 (Schottky diode). Stripe on body = cathode. Cathode goes toward the buck converter side.
  2. D1 (LED). Long lead = anode (+). Flat side on plastic body = cathode (–).
  3. C1, C2 (electrolytic capacitors). Long lead = +. White stripe on the can = – side.
  4. The Nano itself. The USB port faces the edge of the PCB labelled "USB"; don't insert it 180° flipped.

Post-Solder Inspection

Before any power is applied to a freshly assembled board, do this:

  1. Visual inspection under good light

    Every joint should look like a shiny, concave fillet, not a dull grey blob. Dull joints are usually "cold"; reflow them. Blob joints often hide a short to a neighbouring pad; flux and re-touch.

  2. Continuity check: battery + to battery –

    With SW1 in the OFF position, probe across the battery terminals. There must be no continuity. If there is, you have a short somewhere on the input side, most often a solder bridge near J1 or D2.

  3. Continuity check: +5V_LOGIC to GND

    Probe the Nano's 5 V pin (in its socket) against GND. There should be no continuity. If there is, you have a short on the 5 V rail; check around the Nano socket pins and the ceramic capacitors.

  4. Continuity check: +5V_SERVO to GND

    Probe pin 2 of any servo header against pin 1 of the same header. There should be no continuity. If there is (most commonly because an electrolytic was installed backwards and shorted), disconnect everything and find it before applying power.

  5. Smoke test: bench supply at 7.4 V, current limit to 200 mA

    If you have access to a current-limited bench supply, this is the moment to use it. Set 7.4 V, 200 mA limit, and switch on SW1 with no Nano installed and no servos connected. The supply should show roughly 30–50 mA of current draw (the buck converter idle). The buck converter output should read 5.00 V at its output pads. If current goes to limit, switch off immediately and find the short.

  6. Install the Nano, repeat smoke test

    Power down. Insert the Nano into its socket with the USB port facing the labelled edge. Power up again; current should rise to about 80 mA (Nano idle plus buck idle). The Nano's onboard power LED should illuminate.

Mounting the PCB to the Chassis

Once smoke-tested and verified, the PCB drops into the chassis on the four standoffs you installed in §04. Secure with four M2 × 4 mm screws from above. Route the eight servo cables into the chassis from the corner cutouts and onto their labelled headers (J2 through J9). The battery exits the chassis through the rear cutout to its external pack.

Warning · Do not power on with servos connected yet

Confirm the firmware is loaded and the servos are centred (in software) before you connect the servo cables to the PCB. An uncentred firmware can swing servos to mechanical extremes the instant power is applied, and you'll be putting bend forces on your legs that they weren't designed for. This is covered in §07.
