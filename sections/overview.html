The Machine in One Page

Eight servos. Four legs. One Arduino Nano. A custom PCB to keep the wiring honest, a battery to keep it walking, and roughly two weekends of patience to make it all behave.

The platform is a small quadruped, sometimes called a "spider robot" because of its splayed posture, with two degrees of freedom per leg. One servo lifts the leg; one servo swings it forward and back. With careful timing across the eight joints, the robot can produce a passable crawl, trot, and turn-in-place. That's all this manual aims for: a robot that stands, walks forward, and responds reliably. The cleverer behaviour is sealed in a separate envelope and opens on Day 01 in Cebu.

System Block Diagram

Before any soldering, hold the whole system in your head. There are three subsystems and you'll be living inside each of them by turns.

Subsystem 01 · Power

A 2S Li-Ion pack (nominally 7.4 V) is the ground truth. From it, a small buck converter on the PCB derives a clean 5 V rail for the Nano and, critically, a separate, beefier 5 V rail for the servos. Eight servos drawing simultaneously is a non-trivial load; do not be the team that powers servos from the Nano's onboard regulator. You'll learn what brown-out looks like very quickly.

Subsystem 02 · Control

An Arduino Nano (ATmega328P) sits in the centre of the PCB. It drives the eight servos via the Servo library, reads from the on-board buttons or external command (when applicable), and runs the gait state machine. The Nano is more than fast enough for an 8-channel software-PWM job; you are not pushed against any computational ceiling here.

Subsystem 03 · Actuation

Eight SG90 servos, two per leg. The hip servo (closer to the body) controls lift; the knee servo controls swing. This is a deliberately simple 2-DOF leg design. It costs us elegance of motion but gains us a build that can be completed in two weekends with hand tools.

Note · A word on the SG90

The SG90 is the cheapest reasonable hobby servo on the market. It is also famously inconsistent: gear quality, deadband, and centre point vary unit-to-unit. We tolerate this on purpose; learning to calibrate around real-world component tolerance is part of the lesson.

What "Done" Looks Like

By the end of this manual you should be able to demonstrate, on a bench, the following four behaviours:

  1. Power on, stand up. The robot lifts itself into a neutral standing pose within two seconds of receiving power.
  2. Walk forward. A simple four-step gait moves the robot forward by approximately one body length per cycle, in a straight line, on a flat surface.
  3. Stop on command. Pressing the on-board button halts movement and returns the legs to neutral.
  4. Individual servo addressability. Through the serial monitor, any of the eight servos can be commanded to any valid angle.

If you can show us those four things at the meet-and-greet on arrival day, you are ready for what comes next.

Prerequisites

This manual assumes you are a second- or third-year undergraduate in Computer Engineering, Electrical Engineering, Electronics, Computer Science, or IT. That is, you should already have, or be willing to quickly pick up:

  • Comfort with a soldering iron and through-hole soldering
  • The Arduino IDE installed and at least one prior "blink" sketch flashed in your life
  • Reading a basic schematic (resistor, capacitor, regulator, pin headers)
  • Basic command of multimeter use: continuity, voltage, resistance
  • Patience for mechanical assembly with small screws and limited tools

If any of these are unfamiliar, especially soldering, start there first. The build will be miserable if you're learning to solder on the PCB itself.

Tip · Pair up early

Many delegates find it easier to work in pairs during pre-training, even at different universities. A second pair of eyes catches mirror-image leg assemblies and reversed servo connectors before they become catastrophic.

Safety, in Brief

  1. Solder fumes. Work in a ventilated space. Fume extractors are ideal; an open window is the minimum.
  2. Lithium batteries. Do not short, puncture, or charge above their rated voltage. Use a balance charger.
  3. Servos under load. Eight servos can produce enough combined torque to bend a leg or pinch a finger. Power off before adjusting mechanical things.
  4. Soldering irons. Tin the tip, return to the stand, do not check temperature with your other hand.
